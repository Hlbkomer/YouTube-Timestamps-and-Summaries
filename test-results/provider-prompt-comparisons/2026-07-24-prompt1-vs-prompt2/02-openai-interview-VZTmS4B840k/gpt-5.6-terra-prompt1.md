OpenAI’s Greg Brockman describes the company’s shift from chat-based AI toward “agentic” personal AI: systems that understand goals, retain context, use tools, and carry out work on users’ behalf. He argues that this transformation will reshape software interfaces, workplaces, scientific discovery, healthcare, and the AI economy—provided users can build appropriate trust and oversight.

**Main Topic: From Chatbots to Personal AI Agents**
- OpenAI’s long-term aim is AGI that acts like a persistent, goal-oriented assistant rather than a simple conversational model.
- The ideal interface is increasingly “no interface”: users should be able to speak naturally to an AI that understands context and accomplishes tasks.
- Rather than forcing people to navigate apps, files, folders, and menus, AI should adapt technology to human intent.

**How Agents Would Work**
- An agent could connect to tools such as email, calendars, Slack, browsers, and file systems to complete multistep tasks.
- Example: while planning an event, an AI could identify attendees, check known dietary restrictions, draft or send emails to others, and create a seating plan.
- Delegation would be configurable:
  - The AI might only draft actions for approval.
  - With greater user trust, it could execute certain actions independently.

**Trust, Control, and Security**
- Brockman stresses that trust is central to the agentic future.
- Users need clear controls, supervision, and boundaries over what an AI can access and do.
- OpenAI sees trust as something systems must earn through reliable performance and appropriate transparency—not something automatically granted.

**Codex as an Early Agent Platform**
- Codex is presented as more than a coding product: OpenAI employees, including nontechnical staff, increasingly use it for general workplace tasks.
- Brockman says Codex usage inside OpenAI has reached a level comparable to Slack.
- Earlier ChatGPT plugins failed because models had too little memory, context, and tool-use capability; newer models can access far more tools and larger contexts.

**Relationship With Apps and Platforms**
- OpenAI does not frame its product primarily as an operating system, though it could resemble one by becoming a central interface for interacting with many services.
- Brockman expects a broad ecosystem in which software companies benefit when AI becomes better at using their products, rather than being displaced by it.
- He argues that companies that block AI access risk losing activity, while those that support agents could see more usage.

**Devices and Voice Interfaces**
- OpenAI is reportedly working on devices, but Brockman emphasizes that the AI itself is not tied to one device; phones, computers, voice, text, and other channels would be ways to access the same agent.
- Future voice AI should support fluid, overlapping conversation rather than rigid “turn-taking” where users must wait for the model to finish speaking.
- Natural voice interaction could be especially valuable for giving detailed feedback and directing work agents.

**Model Progress and Scaling**
- Brockman rejects the idea that language models are nearing a hard capability ceiling.
- He says scaling—more compute, data, and better architectures—has continued to work, and apparent slowdowns have generally been technical or implementation problems.
- Building frontier systems remains difficult and expensive, requiring deep engineering across hardware, networking, and infrastructure.

**Compute, Competition, and Pricing**
- He expects compute to remain scarce as demand for AI agents grows far beyond current adoption.
- OpenAI is investing in infrastructure and its own chip program because compute takes years to secure and deploy.
- While AI prices will continue to decline for a given level of capability, frontier intelligence will remain premium-priced as better models keep arriving.
- He believes there is room for many companies in the stack—compute providers, model developers, domain-specific software firms, and enterprises.

**Enterprise AI and Differentiation**
- Intelligence is not one-dimensional: models need domain-specific experience and workflows to become truly useful in areas such as finance, legal work, sales, science, and regulated industries.
- Enterprises increasingly want measurable ROI, spend controls, observability, and production-ready tooling—not just access to models.
- Brockman argues that models, compute, and company-specific AI systems all retain value; no single layer will become irrelevant.

**AI in Science and Healthcare**
- Brockman highlights AI’s potential to accelerate scientific discovery, including chemistry, physics, and medical diagnosis.
- He cites cases where OpenAI models helped identify diagnoses that had eluded doctors for years.
- He expects AI-supported health analysis to become standard, helping patients interpret scans and diagnostics, investigate conflicting medical opinions, and explore treatment options.
- He views AI as a way to empower patients, reduce clinician burnout, improve prevention, and advance personalized medicine—while emphasizing the need to deploy it responsibly.
