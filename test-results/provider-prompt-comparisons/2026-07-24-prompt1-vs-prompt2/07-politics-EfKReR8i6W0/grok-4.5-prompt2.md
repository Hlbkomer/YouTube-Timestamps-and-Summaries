Colonel Lawrence Wilkerson, former chief of staff to the U.S. Secretary of State, joins host Glenn Diesen to assess the rapidly escalating conflict with Iran alongside the war involving Russia. Wilkerson portrays Iran as unified, resolute, and strategically sophisticated while describing the Trump administration as incoherent, poorly advised, and locked into a failing strategy of bombing and blockade that risks global economic catastrophe and further nuclear proliferation.

**Iran’s strategy and U.S. leadership failures**
- Wilkerson sees Iranians as unified across institutions with no meaningful internal divisions between hardliners and moderates; they are absorbing daily attacks he calls war crimes yet remaining determined.
- He contrasts this with Donald Trump as indecisive and trapped, surrounded by unhelpful figures such as Pete Hegseth, who publicly claims the U.S. is bringing Iran to its knees and forcing negotiations.
- Trump’s renewed blockade rhetoric and doubling-down on airstrikes are presented as signs of lost strategic reasoning, having already foreclosed softer exit options such as a memorandum of understanding that would have amounted to a masked admission of defeat.

**Missile capabilities, targeting tiers, and Red Sea risks**
- With sophisticated Chinese satellite support (and probable Russian backups), Iran can deliver advanced ballistic missiles across the region with high precision, as illustrated by a strike that destroyed an F-35 inside a hangar in Jordan seconds after it entered.
- Iran has progressed through initial target lists and is beginning higher-echelon economic targets (including Fujairah in the UAE, Red Sea oil infrastructure, and potential strikes on facilities such as Ras Tanura); further tiers could trigger global depression by disrupting commodity flows.
- A prior tabletop exercise concluded the Red Sea/Suez route is now more strategically vital than the Persian Gulf for worldwide shipments of food, oil, gas, and other goods; rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope sharply raises costs and inflation with few viable workarounds.
- Houthi actions have already constrained Red Sea traffic; Wilkerson expects U.S. efforts to reopen it will again fail, compounding the crisis. Iranian-backed pressure is also degrading Kurdish and other potential U.S. partners while Gulf states appear increasingly hollowed out and vulnerable.

**Broader economic, nuclear, and alliance dangers**
- Disruption of maritime flows risks diesel shortages, agricultural inputs, rare-earth and electronics supply chains, and defense production in the United States and elsewhere; China is positioned to gain via rail alternatives.
- Announced U.S.-Saudi nuclear cooperation is framed as a desperate bid by Trump to retain regional relevance and by Mohammed bin Salman to keep ties to a fading American presence; it risks rapid weaponization and regional proliferation after the earlier JCPOA had largely contained the Iranian nuclear issue.
- Wilkerson believes Iran may already possess or be close to a nuclear capability and notes the asymmetric vulnerability of Israel compared with Iran’s size and terrain. Gulf monarchies face possible internal collapse, absorption, or regime change if the war continues.

**Domestic U.S. politics, hatred-driven policy, and military decline**
- Drawing a parallel to LBJ’s Vietnam trap, Wilkerson argues Trump is constrained by domestic politics and faces a collapsing administration that will be among the most reviled in U.S. history, with midterm risks and even civil-conflict dangers.
- Provisions in the NDAA are said to legalize intelligence-sharing practices once associated with Jonathan Pollard’s treason. Bipartisan congressional hatred of Iran and Russia, rather than coherent strategy, drives policy.
- U.S. allies increasingly resemble vassals whose subordination deepens their own insecurity; lack of political imagination prevents crafting a manageable multipolar order.
- Climate and food shocks intensified by the conflict could drive mass northward migration and border militarization. Wilkerson and peers who spent decades in the security establishment express despair at the U.S. military’s ethical contamination, sycophantic leadership, revolving-door corruption, and use as a domestic instrument, viewing the overall trajectory as a profound betrayal of earlier standards.
