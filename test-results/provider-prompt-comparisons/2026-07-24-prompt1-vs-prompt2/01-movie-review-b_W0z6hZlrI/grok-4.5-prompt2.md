**Christopher Nolan’s *The Odyssey* is a technically ambitious, bitterly divisive film that left the reviewer largely unmoved.** For all the culture-war noise around it, he found it exactly what he expects from late-stage Nolan: impressive craft paired with emotional sterility, cold characters, clunky dialogue, and an ending that fails to deliver payoff. His verdict is a flat “meh”—a movie he will likely never rewatch or think about again.

**Plot and structure**
- The story picks up roughly 20 years after Troy. Matt Damon’s Odysseus lives on a beach with Charlize Theron’s character, memory gone; Zendaya occasionally stares at him as a framing device that fills in the past through flashbacks.
- Flashbacks cover the Trojan Horse ending the siege, a disastrous homeward route that leads to the Cyclops (and Poseidon’s wrath after Odysseus cannot resist taunting it), deadly encounters the reviewer likens to “medieval space marines,” Circe turning men into pigs, a trip to the underworld, the forbidden cattle, shipwreck, and seven years kept drugged and ignorant before Odysseus finally builds a raft and luckily reaches Ithaca.
- Meanwhile Anne Hathaway’s Penelope fends off impatient suitors who want the throne. The finale brings reunion, vengeance, and a brutal fight the reviewer found flat and hard to follow.

**Casting controversies**
- Elliot Page, Lupita Nyong’o, and Zendaya appear; the reviewer says their screen time is minimal and largely irrelevant, though he notes Page looks ridiculous in armor and Zendaya barely acts.
- He compares the principle objection to being told food was only spat in “a little,” but insists these casting choices did not shape his overall view.
- He singles out John Leguizamo as a blind old servant in a bad white wig as especially odd casting when an actual older actor could have been hired.
- Robert Pattinson’s antagonist is the rare performance that lands: an obnoxious, cowardly figure the audience genuinely hates—problematic, the reviewer argues, when the villain is the only character who inspires feeling.

**Core creative problems**
- Nolan’s insistence on a grounded, “believable” tone strips away mythology, wonder, and romance, turning the epic into something clinical and bleak—like a beautiful sculpture reduced to a formula.
- Odysseus is written as cold, distant, and android-like: decisions suggest adventure and recklessness, but emotion and dialogue show no urgency, love for family, or desire to reclaim his kingdom. Trauma and guilt over Troy further rob him of agency; he seems to wait for the next punishment rather than strive for home.
- Reunion with son, wife, dog, and revenge on the suitors should be cathartic; instead they feel procedural and obligatory. Even the old dog’s death barely registers because Odysseus hardly acknowledges it.
- Dialogue is on-the-nose and clumsy, full of American accents, modern slang, and swearing that jar against the material (“On this fucking beach—let’s go home”).
- Gods are boring; Zendaya’s is essentially Odysseus’s conscience rather than an active force.

**Craft, look, and final judgment**
- The reviewer praises heavy use of practical effects and real locations as a vanishing commitment in modern Hollywood, but notes the washed-out, overcast, windswept look feels more like Scotland in November than the eastern Mediterranean—matching the film’s relentlessly dour tone.
- He compares over-the-top praise to the hysteria around *Black Panther*, driven by fear of seeming unsophisticated or by culture-war point-scoring.
- Overall: an impressive, thought-provoking, well-cast film by a major director that will likely make money and win awards, yet remains an adventure without spirit, a hero’s journey without a hero, and an epic without grandeur—technically skilled work from someone who can analyze the source but lacks the romantic soul to bring it alive.
