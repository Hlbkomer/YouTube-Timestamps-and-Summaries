The reviewer gives Christopher Nolan’s *The Odyssey* a muted “meh” verdict. He sees it as technically ambitious and visually impressive, but emotionally sterile, poorly paced, and unable to convey the wonder, humanity, or grandeur he associates with Homer’s story. He argues that the extreme praise and condemnation surrounding the film obscure a more mixed reality: it is neither the cultural abomination nor the masterpiece its most passionate supporters and detractors claim.

**Story as described by the reviewer**

- The film begins roughly 20 years after Troy’s fall, with Matt Damon’s Odysseus stranded on a beach beside Charlize Theron’s character and lacking memories of how he arrived there. The narrative then fills in his journey through flashbacks.
- The flashbacks cover the Trojan Horse and Troy’s fall, Odysseus’s decision not to take the straightforward route home, the Cyclops encounter and Poseidon’s anger, Circe turning his crew into pigs, a journey into the underworld, the forbidden cattle, the destruction of his ship, and Odysseus spending seven years trapped with Theron’s character before building a raft and returning to Ithaca.
- Meanwhile, Anne Hathaway’s Penelope resists suitors who want to marry her and take Ithaca’s throne. The reviewer frames the eventual homecoming, reunion, and conflict with the suitors as the intended climax, but says the film does not make it emotionally satisfying.

**Controversy and casting**

- The reviewer says that Elliot Page, Lupita Nyong’o, and Zendaya became major points of online controversy, but argues that each has only a few minutes of screen time and little impact on the overall story. He says their presence did not affect his own opinion of the film.
- He particularly mocks Page’s appearance in armor and says Zendaya “doesn’t even act so much as exists on screen,” though he later acknowledges that her character is the only god to take physical form.
- He also criticizes John Leguizamo’s casting as Odysseus’s elderly blind servant, arguing that a white wig does not convincingly age him and that the production could have cast an older actor instead.
- In the reviewer’s view, online reactions resemble the “borderline hysteria” around *Black Panther*: some critics of Nolan may fear being dismissed as unsophisticated, while some culture-war partisans defend the film primarily to oppose their opponents.

**Main criticism: a grounded approach drains the mythic material**

- The reviewer says he was bored not by the source material, episodic structure, or runtime, but because Nolan’s realistic, grounded approach prevents the audience from connecting to the world and characters.
- He argues that turning mythological material into something restrained and believable strips away the source’s wonder and mysticism. He compares the result to reducing a beautiful sculpture to a mathematical formula: precise and impressive, but not emotionally stirring.
- He says the gods are especially uninteresting, with Zendaya’s figure functioning more like Odysseus’s conscience than a meaningful participant in the story.

**Odysseus and the emotional core**

- The reviewer’s central complaint is that Damon’s Odysseus is written as cold, distant, and emotionally unresponsive. His choices suggest curiosity and recklessness, but his dialogue and behavior do not convey those qualities.
- He says the film fails to establish Odysseus as a devoted husband and father, a king eager to reclaim his home, a clever adventurer, or a compassionate leader. As a result, the reviewer says he did not care much whether Odysseus returned to Ithaca.
- The film appears, in his reading, to portray Odysseus as a traumatized veteran haunted by Troy and by guilt over the sack of a city—an interpretation the reviewer suggests may not come from Homer’s original story. He believes this makes the character too passive: Odysseus seems to endure punishments rather than actively pursue home.
- Because that emotional foundation is weak, the reviewer says the reunion with Odysseus’s son and wife, his revenge on the suitors, and even the death of his old dog after recognizing him fail to land. He describes the finale as a dark, procedural fight in which the action is hard to see.

**Performances, dialogue, and presentation**

- The reviewer singles out Robert Pattinson as the one performer who fully commits, making his antagonist convincingly obnoxious and cowardly. However, he considers it a problem that the villain is the only character who inspires a strong emotional response.
- He criticizes the dialogue for combining modern American accents, slang, and profanity with clumsy, overly explicit lines. A quoted example—“On this fucking beach, let’s go home”—is offered as evidence that Nolan should have written better dialogue.
- He praises Nolan’s practical effects and use of real locations, calling that degree of practical filmmaking increasingly rare in Hollywood.
- Even so, he says the locations do not resemble the eastern Mediterranean. Their washed-out palette, grey skies, and windswept terrain instead remind him of “Scotland in November.”

**Final assessment**

- The reviewer characterizes the film as “bleak, dull, muted, and relentlessly drear”: an adventure without spirit, a hero’s journey without a compelling hero, and an epic without grandeur or scale.
- He credits Nolan with technical skill, ambition, intellectual rigor, a major cast, and a likely future of commercial success and awards attention.
- But he concludes that Nolan lacks the romanticism, sense of wonder, and human feeling needed to bring *The Odyssey* to life. Despite finding it impressive and thought-provoking in some respects, he expects never to rewatch it or think about it again, which he considers his strongest condemnation.
